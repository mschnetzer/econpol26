Historical concepts

Central banks go back to the 17th century. Some rulers relied on them for financing, others to regulate the financial system. Until the mid-20th century, most countries did not have a central bank. Almost all had mints that monopolized money production though.

  • Government bank: Issues currency.

  • Monetary policy: Influence interest rates.

  • Bankers’ bank: Holds deposits of private banks.

Bankers’ bank

The central bank hardly interacts with individual customers. It mostly takes deposits, extends credit and auctions off bonds to financial institutions.

  • Lender of last resort: Lends money to keep credit market going.

  • Manages payments system: With bank notes ensure that they are circulated, with economic payments ensure they clear.

  • Oversees commercial banks: Regulate institutions, inspect banks, stress-test system.

What the ECB cannot do

The ECB is tasked with monetary policy, but not fiscal policy.

It cannot determine, and should not influence, budgets or economic policy.

It does not collect taxes or generate income beyond seignorage

Stability

Financial markets tend to:

  • Episodes of high volatility.

  • Vicious cycles of increased risk-taking.

  • Financial instability.

  • International imbalances.

“The central bank is essentially part of the government.” (Cecchetti and Schönholtz 2021)

Central banks are tasked with stabilizing the system, expressed in interest rates, price levels, exchange rates, even employment and economic growth.

Inflation

Traditionally, price stability has been the primary objective of central banks (e.g. ECB). They are supposed to curb price effects of fiscal expansion, import price, profit or wage pressure.

  • Interest rates: Central banks make lending more expensive, curbing capital accumulation and wage growth, decreasing demand.

  • Lending: Credit freezes lead to deflation, central banks step in to re-start the lending cycle.

Central bank independence

  • No short term policy goals that outweigh long term development.

  • No electoral pressure to increase fiscal spending with danger of demand outrunning supply.

  • No democratic oversight or democratic legitimacy.

  • Recruits from finance or academia with ingrained class interest.

Eurosystem ECB + 21 euro area NCBs · Frankfurt

Governing Council

Main decision body: sets monetary policy (key interest rates, liquidity, reserves). 6 + 21 members; 21 votes at a time: the Executive Board always votes, NCB governors rotate.

Executive Board

6 members: President Christine Lagarde, Vice-President Boris Vujčić + 4. Appointed by the European Council, 8-year non-renewable terms. Implements policy, runs the ECB day to day.

Supervisory Board

Single Supervisory Mechanism (since 2014). ECB directly supervises the large, systemic banks; NCBs and national authorities handle smaller banks.

European Central Bank

The European Central Bank oversees a system of national central banks through anonymous votes of the governing council. Its role and independence is largely codified in the Maastricht accords. Its primary objective is price stability. Secondary objectives include supporting growth and employment as long as it does not run counter to price stability.

  • Owned by the NCBs.

  • Carries out monetary policy (relationships with commercial banks) through the NCBs.

  • Sells and purchases securities but also supplies collateralized lending to banks.

The Austrian National Bank

OeNB was founded in 1923, operating as Austria’s central bank and allocating foreign economic assistance to Austria.

It is an Aktiengesellschaft, with all shares owned by the Republic of Austria since 2010. Before 2010 it was co-owned by the Republic, Wirtschaftskammer and Arbeiterkammer.

The OeNB and Financial Markets Authority (FMA) participate in the European Central Bank as well as the European Systemic Risk Board.

Division of labor

ECB

  • Target interest rates

  • Central analysis

  • Since 2014: single supervisory mechanism for larger banks

NCBs

  • Operate monetary policy

  • Issue and administer banknotes

  • National communication

  • Supervise smaller banks and non-Eurosystem tasks.

Central banks’ balance sheets

Assets

  • Securities, mostly treasury issued, since 2007 increasingly risk assets.

  • Foreign currency reserves to stabilize exchange rates.

  • Loans to commerical banks.

Liabilities

  • Currency: all issued bank notes are redeemable.

  • Government account: The government’s bank account.

  • Commercial bank deposits and reserves.

Central bank transactions

  • Open market operations: Buy/sell securities

  • Foreign exchange intervention: Buy/sell currency reserves.

  • Extend loans to commercial banks.

  • Pay out cash to account holders (government or banks).

Unconventional monetary policy

When the global financial crisis of 2007-2009 unfurled, both the Fed and the ECB introduced new central bank instruments dubbed unconventional monetary policy. They are usually legitimized by conventional interest rates approaching the zero lower bound.

This included targeted asset purchases (e.g. of mortgage backed securities) rather than broad interventions; targeted long-term refinancing operations as cheap credit to warm up credit freezes; and most famously, quantitative easing

Quantitative easing

Quantitative easing supplies reserves to commercial banks beyond the assumed supply/demand relationship that sets the target rate.

The goal of quantitative easing is to supply firms and consumers with money through bank operations.

Geometries

Grammar of graphics

In this course, we mainly work with the ggplot2 R package. The gg stands for Wilkinson’s grammar of graphic. For now it will suffice to understand the basic categories of this grammar.

  • Variables secribe the underlying data structure.

  • Algebra are operations that combine variables.

  • Geometry create graphical shapes from variables.

  • Aesthetics assign data to dimensions of the data - e.g. x- and y-axis, color, or size.

  • Statistics are functions that change the appearance of geometries.

  • Scales represent variables in different dimensions (e.g. the stretching of axis or color palettes).

  • Coordinates are a mapping of graphs onto the 2 dimensional representation we perceive as plots.

Geometries

Geometries (in ggplot2: geoms) are an intuitive concept: The shapes we choose to visualize.

library(tidyverse)   # includes lubridate
library(eurostat)    # install.packages("eurostat")

# euro area members (ECB country codes)
ea    <- c("AT","BE","BG","CY","DE","EE","ES","FI","FR","GR","HR",
           "IE","IT","LT","LU","LV","MT","NL","PT","SI","SK")
cc    <- paste(ea, collapse = "+")
start <- "2015-01"

# helper: ECB Data Portal API -> tibble
ecb_get <- function(flow, key, start = "2015-01") {
  url <- paste0("https://data-api.ecb.europa.eu/service/data/", flow, "/", key,
                "?format=csvdata&startPeriod=", start)
  read_csv(url, show_col_types = FALSE) |>
    select(REF_AREA, TIME_PERIOD, OBS_VALUE)
}

# 1. ECB deposit facility rate (daily -> monthly average)
dfr <- ecb_get("FM", "D.U2.EUR.4F.KR.DFR.LEV", start) |>
  mutate(date = floor_date(as.Date(TIME_PERIOD), "month")) |>
  group_by(date) |>
  summarise(dfr = mean(OBS_VALUE, na.rm = TRUE), .groups = "drop")

# 2. HICP inflation, annual rate of change
inflation <- ecb_get("HICP", paste0("M.", cc, ".N.000000.4D0.ANR"), start) |>
  transmute(country = REF_AREA, date = ym(TIME_PERIOD), inflation = OBS_VALUE)

# 3. Cost of borrowing for house purchase, new business
mortgage <- ecb_get("MIR", paste0("M.", cc, ".B.A2C.AM.R.A.2250.EUR.N"), start) |>
  transmute(country = REF_AREA, date = ym(TIME_PERIOD), mortgage_rate = OBS_VALUE)

# 4. Unemployment rate, seasonally adjusted (Eurostat uses EL for Greece)
unemployment <- get_eurostat(
    "une_rt_m",
    filters = list(geo = recode(ea, GR = "EL"), s_adj = "SA",
                   age = "TOTAL", sex = "T", unit = "PC_ACT"),
    time_format = "date") |>
  rename_with(~ "date", any_of(c("time", "TIME_PERIOD"))) |>
  transmute(country = recode(geo, EL = "GR"), date, unemployment = values) |>
  filter(date >= ym(start))

# combine into one panel
panel <- inflation |>
  left_join(mortgage,     by = c("country", "date")) |>
  left_join(unemployment, by = c("country", "date")) |>
  left_join(dfr,          by = "date") |>
  mutate(period = case_when(
    date < as.Date("2022-07-01") ~ "Negative rates",   # first hike July 2022
    date < as.Date("2024-06-01") ~ "Tightening",       # first cut June 2024
    TRUE                         ~ "Easing"
  ) |> factor(levels = c("Negative rates", "Tightening", "Easing"))) |>
  select(date, country, period, dfr, inflation, mortgage_rate, unemployment) |>
  arrange(country, date)

write_csv(panel, "ea_panel.csv")   # saves a copy so you don't re-download each render

Bar Charts for comparing mean inflation rates between countries.

geom_bar

panel |> 
  group_by(country) |> 
  summarize(inflation=mean(inflation, na.rm=TRUE)) |> 
  mutate(country=fct_reorder(country, inflation)) |> 
  ggplot() +
  geom_col(aes(y=country, x=inflation, 
               fill=(country=="AT"))) +
  geom_text(aes(y=country, 
                x=inflation + .1,

            family="robotoslab",
            hjust = 0,
            color=pal[["grey800"]],

  geom_vline(xintercept=mean(panel$inflation, na.rm=TRUE),
