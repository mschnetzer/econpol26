Central Banks, Banks, Firms and Households

Conventional Central Bank Policy

Interest rates

  • Central banks mostly target interest rates through [very short-time] credit (overnight).

  • The target is a range of auction results.

  • Lower interest rates should cheapen investment, consumption, exports.

Exchange rates

  • Appreciating a currency makes imports cheaper and exports more expensive.

  • Exchange rates are targeted through supply-demand pricing, by releasing or buying up own currency.

  • However, interest rate interventions also change the exchange rate, lower interest decreases demand for domestic assets and thereby domestic currency.

Multiplicators of Central Bank Policy

Actual firm and consumer decisions are not very sensitive to short-term interest rates. Much investment is self-financed, consumption requires saved down payments.

Central bank policy can however be effective through multipliers, that is, someone else amplifying the effects.

Bank Lending Channel

Banks are the main financing channel for firms that cannot afford to issue stock or bonds. Banks’ decisions not to loan money are often the end of story.

  • Central banks changing the lending environment leads to firms receiving funds that would otherwise have received zero funds (not more expensive loans).

  • Open market operation: central bank buys securities from commercial bank, commercial bank earns less income on securities, has to lend more for revenue.

  • Higher level interventions include changing regulation to make sure banks can continue loaning during financial contraction.

Balance Sheet Channel (Credit Worthiness)

Monetary policy can also change the (apparent) credit-worthiness of households, increasing the credit available to them, and thereby consumer demand.

  • Expansionary policy increases (inflates) asset values, which can be leveraged in loans.

  • Lower interest rates decrease repayment burden, increasing profits, firm valuations and firm-related assets.

Asset Price Channel

A fall in the interest rate increases (inflates) prices of stock and real estate.

Fundamental stock prices are assumed to be the present value of future income minus credit cost, the latter factor decreases.

On top of that, expectations of resale prices drive stock prices, which also increase when credit cost falls.

Borderline investment projects become profitable when credit cost decreases, increasing investment for these operations.

Financial Crisis

In financial crisis, increased bankruptcies and opaque network effects decreases confidence in repayment, almost shutting off credit.

  • This shut off the traditional policy channel for central bank policy.

  • Unconventional monetary policy included zero lower bound policies, but also strategic purchases of toxic assets.

Financialization

Stockhammer 2010

Financialization (in the economic sense): post-Fordist move towards credit- rather than income-fueled demand regimes after the shareholder revolution.

  • Often, growth rates react positively on aggregate demand.

  • Demand can be debt-financed if financial institutions and regulations allow for it: credit availability, credit worthiness and credit affordability.

  • Banking norms, shadow banking, faster financial markets and liberalization decreased cost of and barriers to credit.

  • Creates two growth model: export-oriented and credit-financed.

Firms

For firms, increased access to financing comes with increased exposure to financial markets: shareholder interests.

  • Shareholder interest is furthered by asset price inflation, prioritizing “downsize and distribute” over “retain and reinvest”.

  • Extreme case: Debt-financed stock buyback programs to increase stock value.

  • Consequences: Crowd-out of investment and wages, studies suggest causal link.

Households

For households, debt-financed consumption subsidizes income. (Housing) asset price inflation increase credit worthiness.

  • Households increase borrowing, mainly mortgages, but also consumer credit and even payday loans.

  • Allows for increase of consumption standards under stagnating incomes.

  • Increases effective demand without increasing wage cost.

Financial sector

Financializtaion challenges conventional central banking, creates more frequent obstructions of the policy channel.

  • Increased importance of the less-regulated shadow bank sector.

  • Key role as lender of last resort.

  • Manage structural deficiencies (wage suppression and demand deficiencies) through consumer credit.

  • Stabilize an increasingly unstable (financial instability hypothesis) and under-regulated (financial liberalization) economy.

Plotting

fred <- function(id) {
  d <- read.csv(paste0("https://fred.stlouisfed.org/graph/fredgraph.csv?id=", id))
  d <- data.frame(date = as.Date(d[[1]]), value = suppressWarnings(as.numeric(d[[2]])))
  d[!is.na(d$value), ]
}

ecb <- function(key) {
  d <- read.csv(paste0("https://data-api.ecb.europa.eu/service/data/", key, "?format=csvdata"))
  t <- d$TIME_PERIOD
  t <- ifelse(nchar(t) == 7, paste0(t, "-01"), t)   # monthly "YYYY-MM" -> date
  data.frame(date = as.Date(t), value = d$OBS_VALUE)
}

# Country averages of OECD immediate (interbank) rates since 2000
cc <- c("US", "GB", "JP", "CA", "CH", "AU", "NZ", "SE",
        "NO", "DK", "KR", "MX", "PL", "HU", "CZ", "ZA")
rates <- do.call(rbind, lapply(cc, function(iso) {
  d <- fred(paste0("IRSTCI01", iso, "M156N"))
  d <- d[d$date >= as.Date("2000-01-01"), ]
  data.frame(iso2 = iso, mean = mean(d$value), sd = sd(d$value))
}))
